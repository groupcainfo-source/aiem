Why a network works better than a directory

A directory gives you a list of names. A network gives you someone who knows what each of them is good at, who has seen them work and who can tell you honestly whether they suit your project. We build our network by profile and reference, and we introduce people only when there is a real project and a reason to believe they fit.

How a match is made

  1. You describe what you need: a skill, a partner or a supplier, and the project it is for.
  2. We translate that into a profile: the skills, the experience, the size and the languages that matter.
  3. We select from the network and, where needed, from outside it, and check references.
  4. We introduce two or three suitable candidates, with the reasons we chose them.
  5. You meet them. We can attend the first meeting, take notes and help clarify scope.
  6. If you decide to proceed, we help you put the cooperation terms in writing.

Who is in the network

  • Mechanical and electrical engineers and technical specialists.
  • Business, financial and management consultants.
  • Lawyers, notaries and accountants.
  • Suppliers, manufacturers and distributors.
  • Real estate professionals and clinics.
  • Investors looking for projects.

Each member is reviewed on profile and references. We keep the network focused on a limited number of fields, so that an introduction is always relevant to the project and never a mass mailing to everyone on a list.

For clients: what a good introduction saves you

Finding a reliable subcontractor or adviser on your own costs time, and the cost of choosing wrongly appears later. A short, reasoned list of candidates saves you the search, and a written agreement from the start saves you from the disputes that come from unclear roles. If your question is mainly about reaching customers or suppliers in a new market, see market entry and partnerships.

What a cooperation agreement should say

  • The scope of work and who is responsible for each part.
  • How the price is set and when it is paid, including how changes are handled.
  • Deadlines, acceptance criteria and what happens if they are missed.
  • Ownership of the results, drawings and documents produced.
  • Confidentiality, and what happens to client contacts.
  • How either side can end the cooperation.

Working with other professionals can be as simple as a technical second opinion or as large as a joint bid. For technical work see mechanical engineering and electrical engineering; for projects that need financing, a business plan is usually the first step.

Typical situations where we connect people

  • A manufacturer needs a licensed electrician or mechanical contractor for an installation, and wants a reasoned shortlist instead of a phone book.
  • A foreign company wants a local accountant and a lawyer who can work together, in a language it understands.
  • An investor with capital is looking for a project with a competent local operator.
  • A small engineering firm wants to join a larger tender and needs a prime contractor to introduce it.
  • A clinic, hotel or property agent wants a reliable partner who sends international clients and handles the coordination.

Red flags when choosing a partner

Whether you find a partner through us or on your own, a few warning signs are worth taking seriously:

  • Promises that sound too good, with no references you can call.
  • Reluctance to put terms, prices or responsibilities in writing.
  • A company that does everything and cannot show recent work in your field.
  • Pressure to pay a large advance before any work is defined.
  • Contacts that cannot be verified, or that change when you ask questions.

How we protect both sides

  • We introduce only when there is a real need and a reasoned fit.
  • Terms are agreed in writing before work starts.
  • We sign confidentiality agreements on request.
  • We do not guarantee outcomes, and we say so before you decide.
