What a business plan is really for

A business plan has two readers. The first is the person who will decide whether to lend, invest or partner, and who wants to see that you understand the market and that the numbers hold together. The second is you: the process of writing it forces you to test every assumption on paper, which is far cheaper than testing it with your own money. A plan built only to satisfy a form tends to fail both readers.

What we put in a plan that a bank or investor will read

  1. Executive summary. One page that states the opportunity, the amount you need and what it will be used for.
  2. Market analysis. Who the customers are, how large and how fast-growing the demand is, who competes for it and how you differ.
  3. Offer and pricing. What you sell, why customers will choose it and how the price relates to cost and to competitors.
  4. Sales and marketing plan. How customers will actually hear about you and buy, with realistic conversion assumptions.
  5. Operations plan. Location, equipment, suppliers, staffing and capacity, with timelines for each step.
  6. Management and organisation. Who does what, and where the plan depends on specific people.
  7. Financial model. Revenue, costs, cash flow, financing and returns, built from the assumptions above.
  8. Risks and scenarios. What can go wrong, how likely it is and what you would do about it.

Assumptions are where plans win or lose

Every number in a financial model rests on an assumption: price, volume, cost, payment terms, the time to reach full capacity. A reader will look hardest at the assumptions that move the result most. We list them in one place, give each a source or a reasoned basis, and show how the outcome changes when they move. A plan that says openly which five assumptions matter most is more credible than one that presents a smooth curve.

The financial model: what it contains

  • Revenue by product or segment, month by month in the first year and yearly afterwards.
  • Fixed and variable costs, separated, so that the break-even point is clear.
  • Investment in equipment, premises and working capital, with timing.
  • Cash flow including taxes, loan repayments and the delay between selling and being paid.
  • Debt service coverage, which lenders check first, and the return for investors.
  • Three scenarios, base, optimistic and pessimistic, with a short explanation of each.

The model is delivered in Excel with open formulas, so that you can change a price or a volume and see the result without calling us.

Where engineering makes the plan stronger

Many plans fail on costs that nobody checked: the real price of the equipment, the utilities a plant needs, the time construction takes. Because our team includes engineers, a production or property project can be costed from a technical basis. Equipment and installation costs come from mechanical and electrical specifications, not from a guess. That makes the plan easier to defend in front of a lender.

Entering a market with a plan

If the plan is for entering the Albanian market from abroad, it also covers how to reach customers, whether to work through a distributor or a local company, and what the legal structure will be. Those questions are linked to market entry and partnerships and to company formation, and we can handle them together so that the plan and the practical steps agree.

What we need from you to start

  • A description of the business idea or project, even if it is rough.
  • Any numbers you already have: quotes, prices, past sales, costs.
  • The purpose of the plan: bank loan, investor, grant, partner or your own decision.
  • The amount of financing you are looking for, if known.
  • The language the plan must be written in.

Mistakes we help you avoid

  • Treating a hoped-for result as a forecast.
  • Forgetting working capital, so that a profitable business runs out of cash.
  • Using one scenario only.
  • Ignoring the time between starting and reaching full capacity.
  • Writing for the bank when the real decision-maker is a partner, or the reverse.

No consultant can guarantee that a loan, grant or investment will be approved. We build the plan so that it meets the criteria lenders and investors commonly apply and so that every assumption can be defended.

Frequently asked questions

Do you guarantee financing?

No, no consultant can. We build the plan to meet the criteria funders look for and to make every assumption credible.

Can you prepare the plan in English?

Yes. We can prepare it in Albanian, English or bilingual.

How long does it take?

Usually 2 to 4 weeks after we receive your data. The timeline is agreed in writing at the start.

Who owns the financial model?

You do. It is delivered in Excel with open formulas, so you can update it without us.

What is the difference between a business plan and a feasibility study?

A feasibility study asks whether an idea is worth pursuing. A business plan assumes you will go ahead and sets out how. We often start with a short feasibility check when the idea is still uncertain.

How many scenarios do you include?

At least three: a base case, an optimistic case and a pessimistic case, with the main assumptions that change between them.

Can the plan be used for a grant application?

Often yes, but grant programmes have their own formats and criteria. Tell us which programme you have in mind and we adapt the structure.

Will I be able to update the financial model myself?

Yes. The model is delivered in Excel with open formulas, and we include a short walkthrough so that you can change inputs and read the results.
